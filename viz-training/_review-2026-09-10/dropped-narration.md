## original view 1 — A growing tube in a box that is not growing

Start with the straight tube at day twenty-three. It is anchored above by the arch arteries and below by the veins, its dorsal mesocardium has broken down in the middle, and the secondary heart field keeps adding cells at both poles so it lengthens inside a pericardial cavity that does not lengthen with it. Held at both ends, free in the middle, growing. It bends.

## original view 2 — Three movements — say them in order

Three sentences, in this order. One: the bulbus cordis swings ventrally, caudally and to the right — that is the D-loop. Two: the primitive ventricle moves dorsally and to the left, so the two future ventricles now lie side by side. Three: the atrium and sinus venosus move dorsally and cranially, so the inflow ends up behind and above the outflow. If you can say those three lines you can draw the loop from memory.

## original view 3 — Where every segment has landed

By day twenty-eight it is an S-shaped heart with a single continuous lumen. Ventricles side by side in front and below, atria side by side behind and above, joined through the atrioventricular canal, with the outflow tract climbing between the two atria. Cut it across and check yourself against the segment colours from the tube scene — nothing has been added or lost, the same five segments have simply been repositioned. That repositioning is the whole purpose of the step, because septation is only short walls between parts that are already correctly parked.

## original view 4 — Cilia at the node decide which way

Go back to week three for the answer. Motile cilia at the primitive node drive fluid to the left, Nodal and then Pitx2 switch on in the left lateral plate mesoderm, and the embryo acquires a left and a right. The heart reads that signal and loops rightwards. This is why a defect in ciliary motility produces a heart that is a mirror image, and why the same patient has sinusitis and bronchiectasis — the cilia in the airway are the same cilia.

---

## original view 5 — When the loop goes the other way, or nowhere

Three ways this goes wrong. Loop the other way and the ventricles are inverted: congenitally corrected transposition, pink at birth but with a right ventricle doing systemic work for life. Reverse the whole plan and you get situs inversus with dextrocardia, usually a normal heart, and about a quarter of the time Kartagener syndrome. Fail to choose a side at all and you get isomerism — two right sides with asplenia and severe cardiac disease, or two left sides with polysplenia and conduction problems. Then answer any case in the same three steps: situs, loop, connections.
