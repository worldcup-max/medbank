The vertebral column is the central bony axis of the body. It carries the weight of the head and trunk, transmits that weight to the pelvis, protects the spinal cord, and still allows movement. Those four jobs explain almost every feature of its design, so learn the features as answers to those jobs rather than as a list.

There are 33 vertebrae in five regions: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 4 coccygeal. The first 24 stay separate and mobile for life. The 5 sacral vertebrae fuse into the sacrum and the 4 coccygeal into the coccyx, because at the pelvis stability matters more than movement.

Seen from the side the adult column shows four curves. The thoracic and sacral curves are primary — they are present in the fetus and follow the shape of the original fetal C. The cervical and lumbar curves are secondary; the cervical appears when the baby lifts its head and the lumbar when it begins to stand. This is a favourite viva question, so tie each secondary curve to the milestone that produces it.

A typical vertebra has a body in front and a vertebral arch behind, and between them the vertebral foramen. Stacked, these foramina form the vertebral canal that houses the spinal cord. The body is the weight-bearing part and grows larger as you descend the column, because each vertebra carries everything above it. The arch is formed by two pedicles and two laminae, and it carries seven processes: one spinous process, two transverse processes and four articular processes.

Some vertebrae are atypical and each is atypical for a reason.

The atlas, C1, has no body and no spinous process. It is a ring, and it carries the skull. Its superior articular facets meet the occipital condyles, and that joint gives you the nodding "yes" movement.

The axis, C2, carries the dens, also called the odontoid process. The dens is the body of the atlas that was donated to the axis in development. The atlas rotates around the dens, and that gives you the "no" movement. A fracture of the dens is dangerous because the cord lies immediately behind it.

C7 is the vertebra prominens. Its spinous process is long and not bifid, and you can feel it at the base of the neck. Use it as the surface landmark for counting vertebrae.

A thoracic vertebra is recognised by its costal facets for the ribs, often as demifacets on the body, and by its long downward-sloping spinous process. A lumbar vertebra is recognised by its massive kidney-shaped body and its short, broad, hatchet-shaped spinous process — built for load, not for range.

Between adjacent bodies from C2 down to the sacrum sits the intervertebral disc. There are 23 discs and together they make up about a quarter of the height of the column, which is why you are measurably shorter in the evening than in the morning.

Each intervertebral disc has two parts. The annulus fibrosus is the outer ring of concentric fibrocartilage, and it is strongest in front and weakest posterolaterally. The nucleus pulposus is the soft gelatinous centre, a remnant of the notochord. The nucleus acts as a shock absorber and spreads load evenly in every direction.

That weak posterolateral annulus is the whole of the clinical story. When a disc prolapses, the nucleus pulposus herniates posterolaterally, because that is the line of least resistance and the posterior longitudinal ligament reinforces the midline. It therefore misses the cord and strikes the nerve root instead.

The commonest levels are the L4/5 disc and the L5/S1 disc, since these carry the greatest load and the greatest movement. A prolapsed lumbar disc compresses the root passing below it, so an L4/5 prolapse usually takes L5 and an L5/S1 prolapse usually takes S1. The patient presents with low back pain radiating down the back of the leg, which is sciatica. Do not say the disc "slips" — it herniates. The disc is anchored to the vertebral bodies and cannot slip anywhere.

The sacrum is a triangular fused bone that wedges between the two hip bones at the sacroiliac joint. Its upper anterior border projects forward as the sacral promontory, an important landmark in obstetric pelvimetry and the anterior limit of the pelvic inlet.

Finally, the level at which the cord ends matters more than any other single fact here. The spinal cord ends as the conus medullaris at the lower border of L1 in the adult, and at about L3 in the newborn. Below that the vertebral canal contains only the cauda equina floating in cerebrospinal fluid.

That is why lumbar puncture is performed at the L3/4 or L4/5 interspace. You find the level with the intercristal line, also called Tuffier's line, which joins the highest points of the two iliac crests and crosses the body of L4. Enter below that line and the needle meets nerve roots that drift out of the way; enter above L2 and you risk the cord itself.
