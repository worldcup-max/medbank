The breast is a modified sweat gland lying in the superficial fascia of the front of the chest. Both sexes have one; in the female it develops at puberty under oestrogen and progesterone and becomes functional in pregnancy. Almost every clinical sign in breast disease is explained by its anatomy, so learn the structure with the signs in mind.

The base of the breast extends vertically from the second to the sixth rib, and horizontally from the lateral border of the sternum to the mid-axillary line. A prolongation of its upper outer quadrant pierces the deep fascia and enters the axilla; this is the axillary tail, also called the tail of Spence. It is the only part of the breast that lies deep to the deep fascia, and because it is often mistaken for a lump or a node it deserves attention. The upper outer quadrant contains the most glandular tissue and is where most carcinomas arise.

The breast lies on the pectoral fascia covering pectoralis major, and further laterally on serratus anterior and the external oblique. Between the breast and the pectoral fascia is a layer of loose areolar tissue called the retromammary space. This plane allows the normal breast to be moved freely over the muscle, and it is the plane a surgeon uses in a simple mastectomy and in which an implant is sometimes placed.

Internally the breast has fifteen to twenty lobes arranged radially around the nipple, each drained by a lactiferous duct. Each lobe is made of many lobules of secretory alveoli. Near the nipple the duct widens into a lactiferous sinus, where milk collects during feeding, and then opens on the surface of the nipple by a separate orifice. Between the lobes is a large amount of fat, and it is fat rather than gland that gives the resting breast most of its size.

Running through the gland from the dermis of the skin to the pectoral fascia are fibrous bands called the suspensory ligaments of Cooper. They divide the gland into its compartments and hold it up against gravity. Remember them, because they explain the most important physical sign in breast cancer.

The nipple lies at about the fourth intercostal space in a young woman, though it is unreliable after childbearing. It contains smooth muscle arranged circularly and is surrounded by the pigmented areola. The areola carries sebaceous areolar glands, seen as small elevations called Montgomery tubercles, whose secretion lubricates the nipple during lactation.

Blood supply comes from four directions. The internal thoracic artery supplies the medial part through its perforating branches, chiefly the second, third and fourth. The lateral thoracic artery and the pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial artery supply the upper and lateral parts. The posterior intercostal arteries supply the deep part. Venous drainage follows the arteries and also passes to the vertebral venous plexus, which is one route by which breast carcinoma reaches the vertebrae and the skull without passing through the lungs.

Lymphatic drainage is the part to know precisely, because it governs how the disease spreads and how the axilla is treated. About seventy five per cent of the lymph drains laterally and upwards to the axillary lymph nodes, and these are staged in three levels by their relation to pectoralis minor. Most of the remainder drains medially to the parasternal nodes, also called the internal mammary nodes, which lie along the internal thoracic vessels. Some lymph from the lower and inner quadrant reaches the abdominal wall and the rectus sheath, and lymphatics cross the midline to the opposite breast, which is why a contralateral node can be involved.

Now put the anatomy and the signs together. When a carcinoma invades the suspensory ligaments of Cooper it shortens them, and the shortened ligament pulls the overlying skin inwards. That is skin dimpling, and it is brought out by asking the patient to raise the arms or press the hands on the hips. When a tumour blocks the subdermal lymphatics the skin becomes oedematous, but it remains tethered at the hair follicles, so the swollen skin pits at each follicle and looks like orange peel. That is peau d'orange. When a tumour involves the ducts near the nipple and shortens them, the nipple is drawn in; that is nipple retraction, and recent retraction in an older woman is a sinister sign, whereas long standing bilateral inversion usually is not. And when the tumour reaches the pectoral fascia the breast loses its free movement over the muscle, so the lump becomes fixed when the patient contracts pectoralis major by pressing on the hip.

Other conditions to have in mind are mastitis and breast abscess, which occur in the lactating breast, usually from Staphylococcus aureus entering through a cracked nipple, and gynaecomastia, the benign enlargement of glandular tissue in the male, which may be physiological in the newborn, at puberty and in old age, or drug induced, or a sign of liver disease.

In lactation, prolactin from the anterior pituitary drives milk production, and oxytocin from the posterior pituitary contracts the myoepithelial cells around the alveoli to drive the milk ejection reflex. The first secretion, colostrum, is rich in protein and immunoglobulin A.

Any new breast lump is assessed by triple assessment: clinical examination, imaging with ultrasound or mammography depending on age, and tissue diagnosis by core biopsy.
