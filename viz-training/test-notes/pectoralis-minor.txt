Pectoralis minor is a small triangular muscle hidden completely beneath pectoralis major. It is easy to dismiss because of its size, but it is one of the most useful landmarks in the whole of the upper limb, because two important structures are described in relation to it.

It arises from the third, fourth and fifth ribs near their costal cartilages, and its fibres pass upwards and laterally to insert into the medial border and upper surface of the coracoid process of the scapula. Some people have a variant arising from the second to fourth ribs instead, so if the ribs do not look right in the dissection, count them again before assuming you are wrong.

The nerve supply is the medial pectoral nerve, from the medial cord of the brachial plexus. This nerve pierces pectoralis minor to reach pectoralis major, so it supplies both. The lateral pectoral nerve usually contributes as well through a communicating loop in front of the axillary artery.

The muscle is wrapped in the clavipectoral fascia, a strong sheet that runs from the clavicle down to the axillary fascia, splitting to enclose subclavius above and pectoralis minor below. The part of this fascia between the clavicle and the muscle is thickened as the costocoracoid ligament. Four structures pierce the clavipectoral fascia, and they are asked for constantly: the cephalic vein passing in, and the thoracoacromial artery, the lateral pectoral nerve and the lymphatics from the breast passing out.

Pectoralis minor, together with the clavipectoral fascia, forms the anterior wall of the axilla, with pectoralis major in front of it.

Its actions are on the scapula, not the arm. It draws the scapula forwards and downwards against the chest wall, producing protraction of the scapula and scapular depression, and it tilts the glenoid downwards. With the scapula fixed by other muscles it becomes an accessory muscle of respiration and raises the third, fourth and fifth ribs, which is why it stands out in a patient in respiratory distress who is bracing the shoulder girdle.

Now the reason the muscle matters so much. Pectoralis minor crosses in front of the axillary artery and divides it into three parts. The first part lies medial to the muscle, the second part lies behind it, and the third part lies lateral to it. The number of branches from each part is the same as the number of the part: one branch from the first, two from the second, three from the third. The first part gives the superior thoracic artery. The second part gives the thoracoacromial and the lateral thoracic arteries. The third part gives the subscapular artery and the anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries. Once you have the muscle fixed in your mind, the whole arrangement of the axillary artery follows from it.

The muscle divides the axillary lymph nodes in the same way, and this is the classification used every day in breast surgery. Level I nodes lie lateral to and below the lower border of pectoralis minor. Level II nodes lie behind the muscle. Level III nodes lie medial to and above its upper border, up to the apex of the axilla. Spread is usually orderly from level I upwards, which is the basis of sentinel node biopsy. In the Patey modified radical mastectomy pectoralis minor is divided or removed precisely so that the surgeon can reach the level III nodes while sparing pectoralis major.

Clinically, a tight or hypertrophied pectoralis minor can compress the axillary artery and the cords of the brachial plexus where they pass behind it. This is pectoralis minor syndrome, one of the recognised forms of thoracic outlet syndrome, and it produces pain, numbness and sometimes vascular symptoms that are worse when the arm is raised above the head. A shortened muscle also holds the scapula in a protracted, downwardly tilted position, which contributes to the rounded shoulder posture and to shoulder impingement.

Remember the neighbours around its insertion. Three structures attach to the coracoid process: pectoralis minor on its medial border, and the short head of biceps and coracobrachialis from its tip. Subclavius lies above pectoralis minor under the clavicle, and serratus anterior lies behind it on the chest wall, working with it to hold the scapula against the thorax.
