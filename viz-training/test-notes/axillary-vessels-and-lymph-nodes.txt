The axilla is the space between the upper limb and the chest wall, and it is best thought of as a pyramid with an apex, a base and four walls. Through it pass the axillary artery, the axillary vein and the cords of the brachial plexus, wrapped together in the axillary sheath, and around them lie the axillary lymph nodes. Learn the container first and the contents become easy.

The anterior wall of the axilla is pectoralis major, pectoralis minor and the clavipectoral fascia. The posterior wall is subscapularis above and teres major and latissimus dorsi below. The medial wall is the upper ribs with their intercostal muscles, covered by serratus anterior. The lateral wall is the narrow strip of the intertubercular sulcus of the humerus, which is why the lateral wall is really just a groove. The apex opens into the neck between the clavicle, the first rib and the upper border of the scapula, and the base is the skin and fascia of the armpit.

THE AXILLARY ARTERY

The axillary artery is the continuation of the subclavian artery, and it begins at the outer border of the first rib. It ends at the lower border of teres major, where it becomes the brachial artery. Those two borders are the whole of its extent, and examiners ask for them by name.

Pectoralis minor crosses in front of the artery and divides it into three parts. The first part lies medial to the muscle, the second part behind it, and the third part lateral to it. The branches are easy to remember because the number of branches equals the number of the part.

The first part gives one branch, the superior thoracic artery, a small vessel to the upper two intercostal spaces.

The second part gives two, the thoracoacromial artery and the lateral thoracic artery. The thoracoacromial artery pierces the clavipectoral fascia and divides at once into its acromial, deltoid, clavicular and pectoral branches. The lateral thoracic artery runs along the lower border of pectoralis minor and is an important supply to the breast.

The third part gives three: the subscapular artery, which is the largest branch of the whole vessel, and the anterior circumflex humeral artery and the posterior circumflex humeral artery, which encircle the surgical neck of the humerus. The posterior circumflex humeral artery accompanies the axillary nerve through the quadrangular space, which is why both are injured together in a fracture of the surgical neck.

THE AXILLARY VEIN

The axillary vein is formed at the lower border of teres major by the union of the basilic vein with the venae comitantes of the brachial artery, and it ends at the outer border of the first rib by becoming the subclavian vein. It lies medial and anterior to the artery, which shields the artery during most approaches. The cephalic vein joins it near the top after piercing the clavipectoral fascia.

The vein matters more than the artery in day to day practice, for three reasons. It is the vessel entered in a subclavian central line. Its walls are held open by the surrounding fascia, so a tear in the neck can draw in air and cause an air embolism. And its tributaries and the nodes around it are removed in an axillary clearance, which is why lymphoedema of the arm follows that operation.

THE AXILLARY LYMPH NODES

There are about twenty to thirty nodes in the axilla, described in two different ways. The anatomical description gives five groups, and the surgical description gives three levels. Know both, because the ward round uses one and the examiner may ask the other.

The five anatomical groups are these. The anterior or pectoral group lies along the lower border of pectoralis minor with the lateral thoracic vessels, and it drains the breast and the anterior chest wall. The posterior or subscapular group lies along the posterior wall with the subscapular vessels, and it drains the back as low as the iliac crest. The lateral or humeral group lies along the axillary vein and drains the upper limb. The central group lies in the fat at the base of the axilla and receives from the first three. The apical or infraclavicular group lies at the apex and receives from all the others, draining onward into the subclavian lymph trunk.

The three surgical levels are defined by pectoralis minor, exactly as the artery is. Level I lies lateral to and below the lower border of the muscle. Level II lies behind it. Level III lies medial to and above its upper border. In the Patey modified radical mastectomy pectoralis minor is divided so that level III can be reached while pectoralis major is spared.

HOW A BREAST CANCER TRAVELS

About seventy five per cent of the lymph from the breast passes to the axillary nodes, and it usually reaches the anterior or pectoral group first. Spread is generally orderly, from level I to level II to level III, and that orderliness is the whole basis of sentinel lymph node biopsy: if the first node to receive dye or tracer is clear, the rest are very likely clear too, and a full axillary clearance with its risk of lymphoedema can be avoided.

Most of the remaining lymph passes medially to the internal thoracic nodes, also called the parasternal nodes, along the internal thoracic vessels. This route is silent clinically and is why a medial quadrant tumour can spread without any palpable axillary node.

When tumour blocks enough subdermal lymphatics the skin of the breast becomes oedematous but stays tethered at the hair follicles, giving the pitted appearance called peau d'orange. When the axillary nodes are cleared or irradiated, the drainage of the arm is destroyed and chronic lymphoedema follows.

One last practical point. Because the cords of the brachial plexus travel with the vessels inside the axillary sheath, local anaesthetic placed in that sheath reaches all of them at once. That is the axillary approach to a brachial plexus block, and it works for exactly the reason the anatomy predicts.
