Pectoralis major is the large fan-shaped muscle of the front of the chest. It is the muscle that gives the anterior axillary fold its shape, and almost everything you need to know about it follows from the fact that it arises from a wide origin and inserts into a narrow one.

It has three heads, named for where they come from. The clavicular head arises from the medial half of the front of the clavicle. The sternocostal head, which is much the largest, arises from the front of the sternum and from the upper six costal cartilages. The abdominal head is small and arises from the aponeurosis of the external oblique. Some books describe the muscle as having only a clavicular part and a sternocostal part, and count the abdominal fibres with the latter, so do not be surprised when the number changes between texts.

All three heads converge laterally and insert into the lateral lip of the intertubercular sulcus of the humerus, which is also called the bicipital groove. The tendon is worth a moment because examiners like it. It is a bilaminar tendon, meaning it is folded on itself into two layers. The clavicular fibres pass to the anterior lamina and the sternocostal and abdominal fibres pass behind them to the posterior lamina, so the lowest fibres of the muscle end up highest on the humerus. That fold is what allows a muscle with a very wide origin to insert into a groove barely a few centimetres long, and it is why the tendon does not tangle as the arm moves through its range.

The nerve supply is from both pectoral nerves, and the naming trips people up. The lateral pectoral nerve arises from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus and supplies mainly the clavicular head. The medial pectoral nerve arises from the medial cord, pierces pectoralis minor and supplies the sternocostal and abdominal parts as well as pectoralis minor itself. The nerves are named for the cords they come from, not for their position on the chest wall. Blood comes chiefly from the pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial artery, with contributions from the lateral thoracic artery and the perforating branches of the internal thoracic artery.

The actions follow the direction of the fibres, and the two main heads pull in different directions. As a whole the muscle produces adduction and medial rotation of the arm at the shoulder. The clavicular head, whose fibres run downwards and laterally, flexes the arm from the anatomical position and is the head you use to bring the arm forwards and across the chest. The sternocostal head, whose fibres run upwards and laterally, extends the flexed arm back down against resistance, which is the movement of chopping wood or of pushing yourself up out of a chair. If you ask a patient to press the palms together in front of the chest, the whole muscle stands out and can be inspected and felt.

With the arm fixed, as when a patient grips the sides of the bed, pectoralis major becomes an accessory muscle of respiration and helps to raise the ribs. This is why a breathless patient instinctively leans forward and braces the arms.

The lateral border of pectoralis major and the medial border of deltoid are separated by the deltopectoral groove. The cephalic vein runs in this groove and pierces the clavipectoral fascia at its upper end to join the axillary vein, in the small triangle bounded by clavicle, deltoid and pectoralis major that is called the deltopectoral or clavipectoral triangle. This is a reliable place to find the vein for venous access and for pacemaker insertion, and it is a standard surface landmark.

Deep to pectoralis major lie pectoralis minor and subclavius, wrapped in the clavipectoral fascia, and behind the whole group is serratus anterior on the chest wall. The pectoral fascia invests pectoralis major itself and is continuous above with the fascia over the clavicle and below with the fascia of the abdominal wall. That continuity matters in breast disease, because the breast lies on the pectoral fascia and a tumour that reaches and fixes to it becomes tethered when the muscle contracts.

Clinically, congenital absence of the sternocostal head with a hypoplastic nipple and short webbed fingers on the same side is Poland syndrome, which is rare but a favourite question. Rupture of the tendon happens in weightlifters during heavy bench pressing and presents with sudden pain, a bulge as the muscle belly retracts medially, and loss of the anterior axillary fold. Because the muscle has a large surface, a dependable blood supply and can be moved without great functional loss, the pectoralis major flap is a workhorse in reconstruction of the head, neck and chest wall. And in a radical mastectomy of the old kind the muscle was removed entirely, which is one reason that operation has been abandoned in favour of procedures that preserve it.
