The pectoral region is the front of the chest wall, and its two named muscles are pectoralis major and pectoralis minor. They lie one on top of the other, they share a nerve supply, and they are almost always examined together, so learn them together. Pectoralis major is large and moves the arm. Pectoralis minor is small, hidden beneath it, and moves the scapula. Everything else follows from that.

PECTORALIS MAJOR

Pectoralis major is the large fan-shaped muscle that gives the front of the chest its contour and forms the anterior axillary fold. It arises from a very wide origin and inserts into a very narrow one, and most of what is asked about it comes from that mismatch.

It has three heads, named for where they arise. The clavicular head arises from the medial half of the front of the clavicle. The sternocostal head, much the largest, arises from the front of the sternum and the upper six costal cartilages. The abdominal head is small and arises from the aponeurosis of the external oblique. Some texts describe only a clavicular part and a sternocostal part and count the abdominal fibres with the latter, so do not be thrown when the number changes between books.

All three converge laterally and insert into the lateral lip of the intertubercular sulcus of the humerus, also called the bicipital groove. The tendon deserves a moment because examiners like it. It is a bilaminar tendon, folded on itself into two layers: the clavicular fibres pass to the anterior lamina and the sternocostal and abdominal fibres pass behind them to the posterior lamina, so the lowest fibres of the muscle end up highest on the humerus. That fold is how a muscle with an origin spanning half the chest inserts into a groove a few centimetres long without its fibres tangling as the arm moves.

The actions follow the direction of the fibres. As a whole the muscle produces adduction and medial rotation of the arm. The clavicular head, whose fibres run downwards and laterally, flexes the arm from the anatomical position and brings it forwards across the chest. The sternocostal head, whose fibres run upwards and laterally, extends the flexed arm downwards against resistance, which is the movement of chopping wood or pushing up out of a chair. Ask a patient to press the palms together in front of the chest and the whole muscle stands out for inspection.

PECTORALIS MINOR

Pectoralis minor is a small triangular muscle lying entirely beneath pectoralis major. It is easy to dismiss for its size, and it is one of the most useful landmarks in the upper limb.

It arises from the third, fourth and fifth ribs near their costal cartilages and passes upwards and laterally to insert into the medial border and upper surface of the coracoid process of the scapula. A common variant arises from the second to fourth ribs, so count the ribs rather than assume.

Its actions are on the scapula, not the arm. It draws the scapula forwards and downwards against the chest wall, producing protraction of the scapula and scapular depression, and it tilts the glenoid downwards. With the scapula fixed it becomes an accessory muscle of respiration and raises the third, fourth and fifth ribs, which is why it stands out in a patient in respiratory distress bracing the shoulder girdle.

Now the reason it matters so much. Pectoralis minor crosses in front of the axillary artery and divides it into three parts: the first part medial to the muscle, the second behind it, the third lateral to it. The number of branches matches the number of the part. The first part gives the superior thoracic artery. The second gives the thoracoacromial and lateral thoracic arteries. The third gives the subscapular artery and the anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries. Fix the muscle in your mind and the whole arrangement of the axillary artery follows.

The muscle divides the axillary lymph nodes the same way, and this is the classification used every day in breast surgery. Level I nodes lie lateral to and below its lower border. Level II nodes lie behind it. Level III nodes lie medial to and above its upper border. Spread is usually orderly from level I upwards, which is the basis of sentinel node biopsy. In the Patey modified radical mastectomy pectoralis minor is divided precisely so the surgeon can reach level III while sparing pectoralis major.

WHAT THEY SHARE

Both muscles are supplied by the pectoral nerves, and the naming trips people up. The lateral pectoral nerve arises from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus and supplies mainly the clavicular head of pectoralis major. The medial pectoral nerve arises from the medial cord, pierces pectoralis minor to supply it, and continues to supply the sternocostal and abdominal parts of pectoralis major. The nerves are named for the cords they come from, not for their position on the chest wall. Blood comes chiefly from the pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial artery, with the lateral thoracic artery and the perforating branches of the internal thoracic artery contributing.

Pectoralis minor is wrapped in the clavipectoral fascia, a strong sheet running from the clavicle to the axillary fascia, splitting to enclose subclavius above and pectoralis minor below, thickened between clavicle and muscle as the costocoracoid ligament. Four structures pierce this fascia and are asked for constantly: the cephalic vein passing in, and the thoracoacromial artery, the lateral pectoral nerve and the lymphatics from the breast passing out. Together, pectoralis minor and the clavipectoral fascia form the anterior wall of the axilla, with pectoralis major in front.

The lateral border of pectoralis major and the medial border of deltoid are separated by the deltopectoral groove, in which the cephalic vein runs before piercing the clavipectoral fascia at the deltopectoral triangle, bounded by clavicle, deltoid and pectoralis major. This is a reliable site for venous access and for pacemaker insertion. Pectoral fascia invests pectoralis major and is continuous with the fascia over the clavicle above and the abdominal wall below. That continuity matters in breast disease: the breast lies on the pectoral fascia, and a tumour reaching it becomes tethered when the muscle contracts.

Remember the neighbours. Three structures attach to the coracoid process: pectoralis minor on its medial border, and the short head of biceps and coracobrachialis from its tip. Subclavius lies above pectoralis minor under the clavicle, and serratus anterior lies behind both, on the chest wall.

CLINICAL POINTS

Congenital absence of the sternocostal head of pectoralis major with a hypoplastic nipple and short webbed fingers on the same side is Poland syndrome, rare but a favourite question. Rupture of the pectoralis major tendon occurs in weightlifters during heavy bench pressing, with sudden pain, a bulge as the belly retracts medially, and loss of the anterior axillary fold. Because pectoralis major has a large surface, a dependable blood supply and can be moved without great functional loss, the pectoralis major flap is a workhorse in reconstruction of the head, neck and chest wall.

A tight or hypertrophied pectoralis minor can compress the axillary artery and the cords of the brachial plexus behind it. This is pectoralis minor syndrome, one of the recognised forms of thoracic outlet syndrome, with pain and numbness worse when the arm is raised overhead. A shortened muscle also holds the scapula protracted and downwardly tilted, contributing to rounded shoulder posture and shoulder impingement.
