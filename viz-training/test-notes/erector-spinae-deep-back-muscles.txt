The muscles of the back come in two kinds, and telling them apart is the first thing to get right. The extrinsic back muscles lie superficially, are supplied by anterior rami or by the accessory nerve, and move the upper limb, not the spine. Trapezius, latissimus dorsi, rhomboid major and rhomboid minor belong to this group, and so do serratus posterior superior and serratus posterior inferior. The intrinsic back muscles lie deep to them, are supplied by the dorsal rami of the spinal nerves, and move the vertebral column itself. If a muscle in the back is supplied by a posterior ramus, it is an intrinsic back muscle. That single rule is worth more than any list.

To reach the intrinsic muscles you strip the back in layers. Take away trapezius and latissimus dorsi first. Under them lie the rhomboids and the two serratus posterior muscles. Remove those and you meet a tough sheet of connective tissue, the thoracolumbar fascia, which is thick and glistening in the lumbar region. Cut through it and the erector spinae is underneath, lying in the paravertebral gutter, the trough between the spinous processes in the midline and the angles of the ribs laterally.

Erector spinae is also called sacrospinalis. It begins below as a single thick tendon, the erector spinae aponeurosis, attached to the sacrum, the iliac crest and the lumbar spinous processes. As it ascends it splits into three parallel columns. Learn them from lateral to medial, because that is how you meet them in the cadaver and how you are asked for them in the viva.

The most lateral column is iliocostalis, and it runs to the ribs. It has three parts named for the regions they reach: iliocostalis lumborum, iliocostalis thoracis and iliocostalis cervicis. The middle and largest column is longissimus, which runs to the transverse processes and, at the top, to the skull. Its parts are longissimus thoracis, longissimus cervicis and longissimus capitis. The most medial column is spinalis, the smallest of the three, running between spinous processes, with spinalis thoracis and spinalis cervicis. A useful memory aid is I Love Spine, lateral to medial: Iliocostalis, Longissimus, Spinalis.

Deep to erector spinae is a further layer running in the opposite direction. These are the transversospinalis muscles, and they pass upwards and medially from a transverse process to a spinous process above. Semispinalis is the most superficial of them and crosses about five vertebrae, and semispinalis capitis is the part that reaches the occipital bone and forms much of the bulk you feel at the back of the neck. Multifidus is deeper and crosses about three vertebrae, and it is best developed in the lumbar region where stability matters most. Rotatores are the deepest and shortest, crossing one or two vertebrae. Notice the pattern: the more superficial the muscle, the more vertebrae it crosses and the more movement it produces; the deeper it lies, the shorter it is and the more it stabilises. The levatores costarum are in the same deep plane and elevate the ribs.

Splenius capitis and splenius cervicis lie in the neck, superficial to the erector spinae in that region but still supplied by dorsal rami, so they are intrinsic muscles despite their position.

What these muscles do follows from the fact that they lie behind the axis of the vertebral column. Acting together, on both sides, erector spinae produces trunk extension and straightens the back from a stooped position. Acting on one side only, it produces lateral flexion of the trunk to that same side. The transversospinal group, running obliquely, produces rotation to the opposite side when one side contracts, and stabilises the column when both do. In quiet standing the erector spinae is almost silent, and it fires hardest not when you are upright but when you are bending forwards and then straightening again.

There is one point about bending forwards that is asked often. As you flex the trunk fully, the erector spinae falls silent and the load is taken by the posterior ligaments. This is the flexion relaxation phenomenon. It is why lifting a heavy load with a fully flexed back is dangerous: the muscles that should be protecting the spine have switched off and the ligaments and discs take the strain instead.

Clinically, this is the region of ordinary back strain. A sudden unguarded movement tears fibres of erector spinae or its aponeurosis, and the patient presents with pain and paravertebral muscle spasm that is worse on movement and eased by rest. Spasm of the muscle on one side pulls the trunk towards it and produces the sciatic scoliosis you sometimes see beside a prolapsed disc. Chronic low back pain often has a large component of deconditioning of these very columns, which is why exercise rather than rest is the modern advice.

Two anatomical facts carry surgical weight. The paravertebral gutter is the plane a posterior approach to the spine uses, and the erector spinae is retracted laterally rather than cut. And because these muscles are supplied segmentally by dorsal rami, a limited injury denervates only a small part of the column and the rest continues to work.
