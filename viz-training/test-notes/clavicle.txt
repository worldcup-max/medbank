The clavicle, or collar bone, is the strut that holds the upper limb away from the trunk. It is the only bony connection between the upper limb and the axial skeleton, and it is the bone you can feel along its whole length under the skin, which makes it a favourite in the osteology viva and in the surface anatomy station.

Hold one and look at it from above. It is not straight; it is shaped like a flattened letter S. The medial two-thirds are convex forwards and rounded in cross section. The lateral third is concave forwards and flattened from above downwards. This double curve is seen only from above, and it is the quickest way to tell a right clavicle from a left. Orient it so the flattened end points laterally, the rounded bulky end medially, and the anterior convexity of the medial part faces forwards: the side it then belongs to is the side you are holding.

There is a joint at each end, and they are quite different. Medially the sternoclavicular joint articulates with the manubrium and the first costal cartilage. It is a saddle joint but it functions as a ball and socket, and it carries a complete articular disc that divides the cavity into two. That disc is the reason the joint almost never dislocates: force travelling along the bone breaks the shaft instead. Laterally the acromioclavicular joint articulates with the acromion of the scapula. It is a plane synovial joint, it is far weaker, and it is the one that separates in a shoulder injury.

The lateral end is held down not by the acromioclavicular joint itself but by the coracoclavicular ligament running from the coracoid process to two marks on the undersurface of the clavicle. The conoid part attaches to the conoid tubercle and the trapezoid part to the trapezoid line. Learn those two names, because they are asked for and because they explain the grading of acromioclavicular joint injury: if the coracoclavicular ligament is intact the displacement is slight, and if it is torn the whole shoulder drops away from the clavicle. At the medial end the costoclavicular ligament anchors the bone to the first rib, and between the two ligaments the bone is left as a free strut.

Four muscles matter on its four surfaces. Sternocleidomastoid arises from the superior surface of the medial third. Trapezius inserts into the superior surface of the lateral third. Pectoralis major arises by its clavicular head from the anterior surface of the medial half. Deltoid arises from the anterior surface of the lateral third. On the undersurface, subclavius lies in the subclavian groove, a shallow furrow along the middle third.

Now the reason all of this matters clinically. Behind the middle third of the clavicle, and separated from it only by subclavius, lie the subclavian artery, the subclavian vein, the trunks of the brachial plexus and, deeper still, the apex of the lung under the cervical pleura. The clavicle is the roof over that crowded space. A displaced fracture of the middle third can injure any of them, and this is also the anatomy behind central line insertion by the infraclavicular route: the needle passes under the clavicle, aiming for the subclavian vein, and if it goes too deep it finds the pleura and produces a pneumothorax.

The clavicle is the most commonly fractured long bone in the body, and it nearly always breaks at the junction of the two curves, which is the junction of the middle and lateral thirds. That point is the weakest because it is where the cross section changes from rounded to flat, and because the strong ligaments at either end transmit force to it. The displacement is worth learning as a picture rather than a list. The medial fragment is pulled upwards by sternocleidomastoid. The lateral fragment is pulled downwards by the weight of the limb and medially by pectoralis major. So the medial piece rides up, the lateral piece drops and is drawn in, and the shoulder appears to sag. Most of these fractures unite well in a broad arm sling without surgery.

Two developmental facts complete the picture, and they are a common pair of questions. The clavicle is the first bone in the body to begin ossifying, in the fifth or sixth week of intrauterine life, and it is the last to finish, with the medial epiphysis fusing at about twenty-five years. It is also the only long bone to ossify chiefly in membrane, by intramembranous ossification, rather than in cartilage. Failure of that process gives cleidocranial dysostosis, in which the clavicles are partly or completely absent and the patient can bring the shoulders together in front of the chest.

Finally, remember the two hollows that take their names from the bone. The supraclavicular fossa above it is where you feel for the enlarged node of an abdominal or thoracic malignancy, and the infraclavicular fossa below it, between deltoid and pectoralis major, is the deltopectoral triangle where the cephalic vein disappears.
