# Failure to Thrive (FTT)

*Dr Ijeoma Stella, Consultant Paediatrician — Paediatrics, topic 22*

## Outline

Definition · Aetiology (organic, non-organic, mixed) · Mechanisms of organic FTT · Psychosocial basis of non-organic FTT · Diagnosis & the growth chart · Which parameter falls first · Investigations · Treatment · Prognosis

## Definition

Failure to thrive is:

- **Weight consistently below the 3rd–5th percentile** for age and sex, OR
- **A progressive decrease in weight to below the 3rd–5th percentile**, OR
- **A decrease across 2 major growth percentiles** in a short period.

## Aetiology

> The physiologic basis of FTT of **any** cause is **inadequate nutrition.** It is divided into **organic, non-organic and mixed** — and **most cases are mixed.**

**Organic FTT** — growth failure due to an acute or chronic disorder that interferes with **nutrient intake, absorption, metabolism or excretion**, or that **increases energy requirements**.

| Mechanism | Example disorders |
|---|---|
| **↓ Nutrient intake** | cleft lip/palate, CNS disease (cerebral palsy), GERD, parasites, pyloric stenosis, rumination |
| **Malabsorption** | coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis, disaccharidase (lactase) deficiency, IBD, short gut |
| **Impaired metabolism** | chromosomal (Down, Turner), fructose intolerance, classic galactosaemia, inborn errors of metabolism |
| **↑ Excretion** | diabetes mellitus, proteinuria |
| **↑ Energy requirement** | bronchopulmonary dysplasia, cystic fibrosis, heart failure, hyperthyroidism, infection |

**Non-organic FTT** — due to **insufficient calorie intake**; usually manifests as **failure to gain weight**, with length and head circumference remaining normal for a time until they too are affected. This is the **most common** pattern.

> **Up to 80% of children with growth failure have no organic disorder** — growth fails because of **environmental neglect (lack of food), stimulus deprivation, or both.**

**Psychosocial FTT** — growth failure accompanies or precedes poor weight gain, thought to arise because **mental stress raises counter-regulatory hormones (corticosteroids, catecholamines)** that oppose growth hormone.

Lack of food may be due to **poverty, poor feeding technique, over-diluted formula (to stretch it), or an inadequate breast-milk supply** (stressed, exhausted or poorly nourished mother). Non-organic FTT is often a **disordered child–caregiver interaction**; the psychologic basis can resemble **hospitalism** (depression from stimulus deprivation → apathetic, anorexic child).

The caregiver may be **depressed/apathetic, have poor parenting skills, be anxious or unfulfilled by the role, feel hostile toward the child, or be responding to external stresses** (other children, marital dysfunction, loss, financial difficulty). But poor caregiving does not explain every case — **the child's temperament shapes nurturance too** (a difficult feeder can uncover a parent–child mismatch).

**Mixed FTT** — organic and non-organic causes overlap: children with organic disease may also have disturbed environments, and severe non-organic undernutrition can produce organic problems.

## Diagnosis

Requires a **thorough medical, family and social history**, a **diet history**, **laboratory testing** and **frequent weight monitoring**.

- Organic FTT can present at **any age**; most non-organic FTT shows growth failure **before age 1 year**, many by **6 months**.
- Plot age against **weight, height and head size** on WHO charts (0–2 yr) and CDC charts (≥2 yr). **Correct for gestation until 2 years** in premature infants.

> **Weight is the most sensitive indicator of nutritional status.**

**Which parameter falls first tells you the cause:**

- Inadequate intake → **weight falls from its percentile before length.**
- Reduced **linear growth (height)** = severe, prolonged undernutrition.
- **Simultaneous** fall of length and weight → suggests a **primary growth disorder or prolonged inflammatory state** (organic).
- **Head circumference falls late** (the brain is preferentially spared) → indicates **very severe or long-standing** undernutrition.

Underweight children may be **smaller/shorter**, with **fussiness, lethargy/sleepiness, constipation**, and **physical (sitting, walking), social and (in older children) pubertal delays**.

> **No single test distinguishes organic from non-organic FTT.** Because both can coexist, search **simultaneously** for a physical problem and psychosocial factors. Evaluation is ideally **multidisciplinary** (physician, nurse, social worker, nutritionist, child-development expert, ± psychiatrist/psychologist), and **feeding behaviour must be observed** with both staff and parents.

**Engage parents as co-investigators** — it protects self-esteem and avoids blaming already-guilty parents. Report suspected neglect/abuse to social services, but **referral for preventive support/education is often more appropriate**. Hospitalised children who **gain weight with proper feeding** are more likely to have **non-organic** FTT (though some non-organic children paradoxically lose weight in hospital).

## Investigations

Extensive testing is usually unproductive. If history/examination don't point to a cause, most experts **limit screening** to:

- **FBC with differential**; **ESR**
- **Urea, creatinine, electrolytes**
- **Urinalysis** (including concentrating/acidifying ability) **and culture**
- **Stool** for pH, reducing substances, odour, colour, consistency, fat content
- Depending on community prevalence: **blood lead, HIV, or TB testing**

Sometimes appropriate: **T4 and TSH**; **growth-hormone assessment** (fasting **IGF-1** and **IGFBP-3**) when height is more affected than weight or both fall together; a **sweat test** for cystic fibrosis if recurrent respiratory disease, ravenous appetite, foul bulky stools, hepatomegaly or family history; review **newborn screening** results. Reserve infection work-up for signs of infection (though a **urine culture** helps as some UTIs lack other signs); reserve radiology for anatomic/functional pathology; check **bone age** if an endocrine cause is suspected.

## Treatment

Three aims: **sufficient nutrition, treatment of the underlying disorder, and long-term social support.**

- Provide a nutritious diet with adequate calories for **catch-up growth — about 150% of the normal requirement**.
- **All children grow when given sufficient nutrition** — so weight gain in hospital does not by itself prove non-organic FTT.
- Organic/mixed FTT → **treat the underlying disorder promptly.** Non-organic/mixed FTT → **education and emotional support** to repair the parent–child relationship, with **referral to community agencies** (a written predischarge plan with clear accountability is ideal; invite parents to a summary session).
- Some cases need **foster care**; return to biologic parents should be based on **demonstrated ability to care**, not merely the passage of time.

## Prognosis

- **Organic FTT** — depends on the cause.
- **Non-organic FTT** — most children **> 1 year** achieve a stable weight above the 3rd percentile.
- Children who develop FTT **before age 1** are at high risk of **cognitive delay** (verbal and maths), **highest** if diagnosed **< 6 months** (peak postnatal brain growth).
- **General behavioural problems in ~50%**; eating/elimination problems in a similar proportion.
