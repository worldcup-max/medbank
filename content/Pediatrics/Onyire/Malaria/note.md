## Epidemiology & local context

Nigeria carries an enormous share of the global burden: **~27% of global cases and ~31% of global deaths.** **Plasmodium falciparum** causes **95%** of cases and is responsible for nearly all severe disease. The vector is the **female Anopheles mosquito.** Transmission is **perennial in the South and seasonal in the North.** The susceptible (at-risk) groups are **children under 5 years, pregnant women, and non-immune adults.**

## Life cycle of the parasite

Three linked stages carry the parasite from mosquito to human and back:

1. **Transmission to human** — the mosquito bites and injects **sporozoites**, which travel to the liver.
2. **Liver (pre-erythrocytic) stage** — the sporozoite infects a **hepatocyte** and multiplies.
3. **Blood (erythrocytic) stage** — the liver cell ruptures, releasing **merozoites** that infect red blood cells; inside the RBC the parasite undergoes the sexual cycle, producing forms that are taken up when another mosquito bites — completing transmission back to the mosquito.

**Pre-erythrocytic stage:** lasts **6–14 days** for P. falciparum, and there are **no symptoms** at this stage. **Erythrocytic stage:** this is where **fever and haemolysis** appear — the clinical signs.

> **Cyto-adherence** is a special quality of P. falciparum: infected red cells **stick to blood-vessel walls (sequestration).** This is one of the key causes of complications.

## Pathogenesis of severe malaria

Severe disease follows a chain of events: **microvascular sequestration → hypoxia → lactic acidosis → cytokine storm → inflammatory response syndrome.** In parallel, **haemolysis** causes **anaemia and splenic dysfunction**, with **haemoglobinuria** and **thrombocytopenia.** Sequestration in the brain produces **cerebral malaria.**

**Organ involvement:** renal/hepatic — mild elevation of liver enzymes, jaundice, hepatic dysfunction. Respiratory — may be normal, or progress to **ARDS.** CNS/pulmonary — **pulmonary oedema** and **severe anaemia.**

## Clinical features — uncomplicated vs severe

| System | Uncomplicated | Severe |
|---|---|---|
| **Fever** | Intermittent; rigor; chills | High grade; continuous |
| **CNS** | Headache; malaise | Impaired consciousness / unconsciousness; seizure |
| **Haematological** | Mild anaemia; thrombocytopenia | Severe anaemia (Hb < 5); DIC |
| **Metabolic** | Mild hypoglycaemia | Hypoglycaemia; lactic acidosis |

## Diagnosis

1. **Clinical suspicion** — based on presentation.
2. **Rapid diagnostic test (RDT)** — detects the **HRP antigen.** Fast and good for P. falciparum. **Caveat: the antigen stays positive for 2–4 weeks after recovery**, so a positive RDT does not always mean current active disease.
3. **Microscopy — the gold standard.** A **thick film** gives parasite density; a **thin film** identifies the species.
4. **Parasite density** — reported per µL (e.g. 100/µL up to 100,000/µL).
5. **Other investigations** — FBC, renal function, blood glucose, LFTs; **arterial blood gas** in severe disease.
6. **Level of care** — RDT at the primary health-care level; microscopy at secondary/tertiary level.

## Management — uncomplicated malaria

- **ACT (artemisinin-based combination therapy)** is the treatment.
- **Antipyresis:** diclofenac or paracetamol suppository.
- **Tepid sponging** with luke-warm water to relieve fever.
- **Supportive treatment:** hydrate/infusion; blood transfusion if needed. (Artesunate should be available if the child deteriorates.)
- **Fever clearance** is expected over about **3 days.** If fever has not cleared, **look for another cause** (e.g. tonsillitis).

> **Reference:** normal temperature of children is **37.2–37.4°C.**

## Management — severe malaria & complications

**Treatment:**

- **IV artesunate is first-line** — continue until the child regains consciousness.
- **Second line: quinine.**
- **Calculate the plasma parasite density.**

**Management of complications:**

- **Airway** — keep it patent.
- **Convulsions** — give an anticonvulsant.
- **Avoid steroids.**
- **Severe anaemia** — transfuse blood, especially if the child is in distress; **transfuse if Hb < 5 g/dl.**
- **AKI** — watch fluid balance.
- **Hypoglycaemia** — check blood glucose **4-hourly** and correct with **10% dextrose.**

> **Rule of thumb:** **Hb ≈ ⅓ × PCV.** Sickle-cell patients need a stable Hb kept where they are comfortable.

## Malaria in pregnancy

- **1st trimester:** **quinine + clindamycin.**
- **Other trimesters:** **ACT.**

## Prevention & challenges

**Prevention strategies:** vector control (insecticide; avoid bushy environments) and **chemoprophylaxis** (Fansidar in pregnancy; mefloquine, doxycycline).

**Challenges:** **drug resistance** in P. falciparum, including **partial resistance to ACT**; **RDT false negatives**; **access to care**; and poor **antimicrobial stewardship** (abuse of antimicrobial drugs).

## Mortality & take-home points

Even when treated, mortality in severe malaria can be high — **fast treatment is essential.**

> **Take-home:** severe malaria with **fever + unconsciousness → think cerebral malaria.**

## Clinical cases

**Case 1 (severe malaria).** A 14-year-old male with 4-day fever, headache and rigor; Hb 6.2 g; RDT positive; parasite density > 100,000/µL. *Why is IV artesunate better than quinine?* — Quinine risks arrhythmias; artesunate gives **faster parasite clearance**, **lower mortality**, and a **better safety profile** (quinine can cause hypotension and hypoglycaemia).

**Case 2 (emergency).** A 4-year-old with fever and seizure; RDT positive but blood film negative. This is an **emergency — severe malaria until proven otherwise.** The RDT shows they have been infected; check for another cause (FBC, LFT, etc.) and **repeat microscopy in 12–24 hours.**
